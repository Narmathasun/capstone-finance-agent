---
title: Understanding Sharpe Ratio
source: Internal Financial Education Team
category: portfolio_analysis
---
The Sharpe ratio is a widely used measure of risk-adjusted return,
calculated as a portfolio's excess return (return above a "risk-free" rate,
like short-term Treasury bills) divided by its standard deviation
(volatility). It answers the question: how much return did an investment
generate per unit of risk taken?

A higher Sharpe ratio indicates better risk-adjusted performance — two
portfolios might have identical raw returns, but the one that achieved
those returns with less volatility (a higher Sharpe ratio) is generally
considered to have delivered a "better" outcome relative to the risk taken.

Limitations: the Sharpe ratio treats all volatility (both upside and
downside) as "risk," even though most investors only truly mind downside
volatility. It also relies on historical data, which doesn't guarantee
future results, and can be skewed by unusual return distributions. It's
best used as one comparative tool among several rather than a standalone
verdict on investment quality.
