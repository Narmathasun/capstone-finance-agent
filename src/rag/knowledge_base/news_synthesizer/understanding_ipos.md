title: Understanding IPOs
source: Internal Financial Education Team

An Initial Public Offering (IPO) is the process by which a private company
first sells shares to the public, becoming a publicly traded company on a
stock exchange. Companies pursue IPOs primarily to raise capital for
growth, provide liquidity for early investors and employees, and increase
public visibility.

IPO news often generates significant media attention and investor
excitement, but newly public companies carry specific risks: limited
public trading history to evaluate, potential volatility as the market
determines a "fair" price with less historical data, and "lock-up periods"
(commonly 90-180 days) during which company insiders are restricted from
selling shares — meaning a wave of insider selling once the lock-up expires
can sometimes pressure the stock price.

Historical data on IPO performance is mixed — some IPOs perform very well
long-term, while many others underperform the broader market in the years
following their debut, making individual IPO investing a higher-risk,
higher-uncertainty area relative to established public companies.
